# Week 1 — The Skill Audit · Instructor Facilitation Notes

**Session:** Week 1 of 8 · ~60 min · 6 students (ages 11–12) · taught solo
**Computers:** None. This is an all-paper session on purpose.
**On-screen title (what the kids see):** *Player One* — "The Skill Audit" is our name for the week, not theirs.

---

## What this hour is really for

Three ideas should be true in the room by the time they leave:

1. **A game = a Goal + Rules + Choices + a Player.** Not graphics. Not code.
2. **Every game is built from four systems — Logic, Art, Story, Sound.** You have a superpower in one; this course grows the other three.
3. **You can design and *test* a game on paper before writing a line of code.** This is what pros do, and it's the whole philosophy of the course: *Designer > Coder.*

**By the end, every kid has:**
- A completed **Player Stat Sheet** with a named **Designer Persona**.
- At least one **Mechanic Card** they built and playtested on the grid.
- A started **Designer's Log** (their folder) with today's date and both artifacts in it.

---

## Room setup (do before they arrive)

- Tables in a cluster or U-shape so all 6 can see the screen and each other.
- One **shared grid** for playtesting: either a 6×6 (or 8×8) grid taped on a table/floor with painter's tape, or a big sheet of 1-inch grid chart paper. Mark a **START** square, a **⭐ GOAL** square, and 1–2 **hazard** squares.
- A "Parking Lot" sheet on the wall for stray ideas/questions you don't want to lose.
- At each seat: 1 Player Stat Sheet, a cup of **10 counting chips**, 3–4 blank index cards, a pencil.
- The game definition written on the board but **covered / hidden** until the cold open lands it.

---

## Timeline

### 0:00–0:05 — Cold open: "Is this a GAME?"
Don't introduce yourself yet. Open the deck and go straight in. Put each item up and take a fast thumbs-up / thumbs-down:

- A jigsaw puzzle
- Tic-tac-toe
- A Marvel movie
- Playground tag
- A slot machine

Let them argue for ~20 seconds each. The useful fights:
- **Movie** — you just watch it. No *choices*. (→ agency matters)
- **Slot machine** — do you actually *choose* anything, or just pull? (→ meaningful choice matters)
- **Tag** — no screen, no code, clearly a game. (→ games aren't about tech)

**Reveal the definition on the board:**
> **GAME = GOAL + RULES + CHOICES + a PLAYER**

This stays up all 8 weeks. Point back to it constantly.

### 0:05–0:08 — The class in one sentence
> "You're not here to learn to code. You're here to design games — and code is just one of the tools we'll pick up along the way. Today: no computers at all. The best game makers in the world start on paper, and so do we."

Name the course arc in a line: paper → digital skeleton → polish → showcase.

### 0:08–0:22 — The Player Stat Sheet (Designer Persona)
Frame it as an **RPG character sheet — but the character is YOU, as a designer.**

Four stats, one line each so they get it fast:
- **LOGIC** — rules, systems, puzzles, how things *work*.
- **ART** — the look, color, feel, visuals.
- **STORY** — characters, world, *why we care*.
- **SOUND** — music, effects, mood.

**The allocation:** each kid has **10 chips**. Spend all 10 across the four stats. **Max 5 in any one stat** — this forces a real choice and stops an all-in dump. Place chips in the four boxes, then write the number down.

**Name your Persona** from your highest stat. Give examples, then push them to invent their own:
- High Logic → "The Architect"
- High Art → "The Visionary"
- High Story → "The Worldbuilder"
- High Sound → "The Composer"

Finish the sentence on the sheet: *"My games will always have great ______."*

**Share-out (30 sec each, use a timer):** persona name + that sentence.

> **Teaching beat:** "Notice — nobody maxed all four. Nobody can. Real studios are *teams* of personas. Your superpower is your starting class; this course levels up the other three."

### 0:22–0:28 — What a game is really made of
Tie the four stats to the four systems *every* game has. Use one game they all know (Minecraft works well):
- **Logic** = gravity, mobs, crafting recipes
- **Art** = the blocks, the look
- **Story** = why you're here, the world
- **Sound** = the music, the "oof," the cave noises

Then land the hinge for the next activity:
> "Under all of it sits one thing — a **mechanic**. A mechanic is a rule the player can *do something* with. 'Jump.' 'Mine a block.' 'Dash.' A good mechanic is a **verb + a limit**. Today we design mechanics — on paper."

### 0:28–0:50 — Paper Prototype Challenge: Mechanic Cards on the Grid
This is the heart of the hour. Keep energy high; keep it moving.

**1. Build a card (5 min).** Each kid writes one **Mechanic Card** on an index card:
- **NAME** — e.g. "Double Step"
- **WHAT IT DOES** — e.g. "Move exactly 2 squares in one direction"
- **A LIMIT or COST** — e.g. "You can't turn mid-move"

Seed with 2 examples so nobody stalls:
- *Hop:* move 1 square any direction, **including diagonal**.
- *Dash:* move 3 squares straight, but **you can't stop on a hazard**.

**2. Playtest (12 min).** Token on START. Pick one kid's card and move toward the ⭐ using **only** that mechanic. The group reacts out loud: Does it work? Is it *fun*? Too easy? Impossible to reach the goal? Cycle through as many cards as time allows.

**3. Iterate (5 min).** "Change **one** thing on your card to make it better," then retest that one. This is the lesson: *testing reveals problems; changing one variable at a time is how designers fix them.*

> **What this teaches:** a mechanic is a verb + a constraint; a goal + hazards create *stakes*; playtesting + iteration is the actual job.

### 0:50–0:57 — The Designer's Log
Hand out the folders.
> "Everything you make in this class lives here. It's the whole story of how your game goes from paper to real — and in Week 8 you'll show this folder right next to your finished game."

Into the log today: the **Stat Sheet** + their **Mechanic Card(s)**. Have each kid write the date and one line: *"Today I learned a game is ______."*

### 0:57–1:00 — Wrap + tease Week 2
Point at the board definition one more time.
> "You just did what every pro does on day one: figured out who you are as a designer, and tested a game mechanic — with zero code. Next week we take a mechanic like yours and map the story around it — Start → Conflict → Victory — the skeleton your whole game hangs on."

## Side-Quests (for the fast finishers — keep depth over speed)

Hand these out individually the moment someone says "I'm done":

- **Teammate Persona:** design a second persona you'd *want on your team* — which stat completes you, and why?
- **Physics mechanic:** add a *momentum* rule — after you move, you must keep moving at least 1 more square (no stopping on a dime). Playtest how it changes the feel.
- **Two-mechanic combo:** write a second card *and* a rule for when each one is allowed. Now you have a mini-system.
- **Evil Designer:** forget the hero — design the *hazards*. Place hazards so that a mechanic that was easy suddenly becomes hard but still *fair*. (This is level design; it's the most popular side-quest — have extra hazard tokens ready.)

---

## Managing these 6 specifically

- **They finish fast and get competitive.** Lean hard on side-quests and the "Evil Designer" role so speed turns into depth, not boredom.
- **Keep share-outs tight.** Use a timer or bell, or one enthusiastic kid eats the clock.
- **One shared grid vs. splitting:** with 6, run **one shared grid** this first week so you can model good playtesting out loud. Split into two trios with their own grids in later weeks once they know the ritual.
- **Watch the self-rating.** Reframe the kid who dumps everything into Logic because "coding = smart," and reassure the kid who rates low everywhere. All four stats are equally "smart" — say it explicitly.
- **If the grid activity runs hot/long,** it's fine to cut the second iteration round and protect the Designer's Log wrap — the log is the thing they take home.
